# Security and Financial Privacy

Security has been defined by the American Heritage Dictionary as, “freedom from risk or danger”. The domain of security discussion spans physical security and information security. While there are many examples of protecting and securing the physical world, it is crucial for us to recognize the importance of analyzing and advancing information security systems. Moreover, progress in information security should run at least parallel to the advancement of technological systems. To add to that, the balance between security and privacy has to be maintained; achieving security and compromising privacy is next to achieving nothing. This paper will walk the reader through the case of information security in the financial services industry and demonstrate the erosion of financial privacy that stems from the legal landscape encountered in the United States, while ensuring national security. Legislation passed after the events of September 11, 2003, is placing immense burden on the financial services industry to comply with national security and privacy laws.

Systems security is difficult to define, much less achieve. Bruce Schneier makes a successful attempt at characterizing systems security in his book, Secrets and Lies. Systems are inherently complex, interact with one another, have emergent properties and have bugs. In the context of this paper, we shall discuss a computer as a system. A computer is not just a machine performing a single, fixed function; it is built of many different machines such as a central processing unit, a video display card, various disk drives and whatever else a computer is made of! A computer certainly interacts with other systems, which is clearly demonstrated by the communication that is taking place between many computers on the Internet every second. Computers have emergent properties. This is better understood by thinking of the impact that computers and the Internet have had on human relationships (by exploiting electronic mail and instant messaging). Indeed, a computer was not designed with this in mind. And of course, computers are “buggy”, a trait of the computer that every avid user has experienced before. Now think computer security or information security for that matter. “General computer security, which can be defined as the prevention and/or detection of unauthorized actions by users of a computer system, seems a whole lot harder than the simple mathematics of cryptography. And it is” (Schneier 120). For those of us who are familiar with encryption and cryptography, we know and will agree that the mathematics of cryptography is not so simple and even if it is, not all problems relating to computer security are solved by cryptography. If that were the case, there would be no such technologies as intrusion detection systems, firewalls, or biometrics and everything could rest on the platform of encryption. Hence, with the complexity of systems increasing daily and the surge in computer viruses and attacks, there is a major need for the development and progress of information security solutions.

The financial services industry is a significant segment of our society and the principle reason behind this is the fact that a major portion of our society has financial assets (or aspires toward attaining them) and wants to manage these assets efficiently and profitably. Financial institutions perform many functions for their customers and these functions drive the need for security. Examples of financial services functions that absolutely require security are electronic transactions and e-commerce systems, protection of network perimeters from hackers and attacks, and access control for employees and internal entities to sensitive data and assets. Once again, it is not difficult to imagine the complexity of the security solutions required for financial institutions to fulfill their functions.

Rather than addressing specific solutions toward specific problems faced by the financial services industry, I am going to tie concepts of computer security using Schneier’s approach and discuss their relevance to the financial services industry. “Historically, computer security has three aspects: confidentiality, integrity and availability” (Schneier 121). Confidentiality is crucial to financial institutions because neither you nor I want our personal and financial information leaked to someone else - be it our neighbor or someone with the intent of identity theft or fraud. Integrity is very important from the point of view that, the terabytes of personal and financial data not be tampered with or modified without reasonable authorization. Availability, in this context, refers to the systems and more importantly, the information security systems be up and running when absolutely required, so as to perform detection if not prevention. It would be ideal to have information security systems preventing and mitigating potential risk! Thus, these key ingredients are necessary for the protection and mitigation of risk for the financial services industry today.

If security is the left-hand side of the equation, then privacy is the right-hand side of the same equation. Privacy is not a simple concept to define. Philip Agre and Marc Rotenberg define it as the following: “Privacy is the capacity to negotiate social relationships by controlling access to personal information.” This definition is the most encompassing with regard to the concept of privacy and the role it plays in our society. We should also keep in mind the more traditional meaning of privacy, which is the exclusive right an individual has over his/her space, time or other belongings. It is apparent that as human beings who are a part of a complex social system, it is easier for us to understand the idea of invasion of privacy versus privacy itself.

Schneier makes an accurate observation about people and privacy. “People have a complicated relationship with privacy. … Almost no one realizes exactly how important privacy is in his or her life” (Schneier 59). Businesses, on the other hand, know the importance of privacy and sometimes go a long way to protect it. However, when cost-cutting and other measures beckon for sustained economic prosperity “security is the first thing that gets thrown out the window” (Schneier 59). Governments are seemingly comfortable with the idea of privacy in terms of military secrets and the like. Unfortunately, in recent times, especially post 9/11, the notions of security and privacy have been mixed up and it is the individual in society who is paying and will pay the price of the jumbled details of security and privacy.

Specific to the discussion presented in this paper, I am going to address the need for financial privacy and how it is being eroded by the new legal landscape. Why financial privacy? The American Civil Liberties Union outlines a few reasons for financial privacy, which are very relevant to our society and make a good case for the justification of financial privacy. Financial privacy can be the means to decrease the power and leverage of insurance companies and other big companies over individuals. More importantly, it will prevent the government from collecting personal information and infringing on our privacy. Financial privacy can also make it difficult for data aggregation companies to compile “informative” dossiers of American citizens and capitalize on this data for various purposes. To a lesser degree, financial privacy will keep the annoying telemarketers, electronic spam and other unwanted marketing at bay.

Swiftly after the events of September 11, 2001, the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled “an act to deter and punish terrorist acts in the United States and around the world, to enhance law enforcement investigatory tools, and for other purposes”. Titled very appropriately as the USA PATRIOT ACT, it really stands for “Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act of 2001”. At that time, emotions naturally ran high and American society scrambled to hold onto anything that might possibly guarantee some form of security. There is no doubt that even if society did not embrace any and every method of enforcing national security, the government successfully mechanized instruments ensuring security, tunneled via the legal landscape.

I appreciate and understand the need for national security and completely respect the government’s proactive approach toward this matter. However, something needs to be said for the privacy of American society as well; I feel that the very fundamentals on which American society has been established are being negotiated for some sense of security. There are dozens of organizations out there, lobbying and fighting for privacy in various aspects of American society. There are hundreds of papers and books written about the neglect of privacy issues in forming the latest legislation. I think and believe that it is not so much the neglect of privacy, but a general lack of understanding of the difference between security and privacy. The next part of this paper dissects a relevant section of the USA PATRIOT ACT with regard to financial aspects and demonstrates the compromise of financial privacy en route to attaining security.

The USA PATRIOT ACT explicitly makes many amendments to previous acts, such as the Right to Financial Privacy Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act and the Federal Deposit Insurance Act. These amendments are equivalent to taking rights away that were once granted to American citizens. In the discussion that follows, the United States Constitution (and its amendments) should be kept in mind. The First Amendment guarantees free speech rights; “Congress shall make no law… abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the government for a redress of grievances” (United States Constitution, First Amendment). The Fourth Amendment guarantees privacy and due process rights; “The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no warrants shall issue, but upon probably cause…” (United States Constitution, Fourth Amendment).

The USA PATRIOT ACT makes a very striking amendment to the Right to Financial Privacy Act of 1978. The main theme of the insertion is that any government authority is authorized to conduct investigations of intelligence or counterintelligence related to international terrorism or for any other purpose so authorized. While investigations related to acts of terror seem totally reasonable, one should note the severity of this amendment. Government authority is very loosely defined and the government essentially dictates when, what and who they wish to investigate. Moreover, extensive analysis of the language used to construct this amendment shows that a government authority may conduct an investigation sparked by the slightest inkling relating to a potential act of terrorism. In my mind, this is a severe amendment to the Right to Financial Privacy Act, which implies that all our financial records, transactions and other financial information is subject to investigation whenever any government authority insists on doing so. This is certainly a bold invasion of financial privacy for any individual!

Another interesting amendment is that made to the Fair Credit Reporting Act of 1970. The central theme of this amendment is that disclosures to governmental agencies for counter terrorism purposes will be fulfilled accordingly; any consumer reporting agency shall furnish a consumer report and all other information in a consumer’s file to a government agency authorized to conduct investigations. Why should the government get hold of my consumer report? It is awfully suggestive that by seeking even consumer reports, the government plans to map the most remote clues available to terrorist activity if possible, all in the bid for national security. Another disturbing feature of this amendment is the fact that an individual will never know that the government requested his or her consumer report. My analysis of such an amendment only leads me to think that such information-gathering implies immense tracking and monitoring of consumer habits and a gross invasion of an individual’s financial privacy; it is scary to think of the possibility of a government agency mapping some “deviant” consumer activity to terrorism and the dire consequences it could have for someone whose intent does not present any danger or risk whatsoever. I also wonder if a consumer’s report will be hurt because of the simple fact that a government agency requested his or her consumer report. This is a valid question since the USA PATRIOT ACT does not address such issues and deliberately leaves them open-ended. It is this very approach toward security that is violating and gradually eroding the broader concepts of privacy in our society.

Yet another amendment of interest is one relating to the Bank Secrecy Act, made to the Federal Deposit Insurance Act. The government claims that adequate records maintained by insured depository institutions have a high degree of usefulness in criminal, tax and regulatory investigations or proceedings. They may also have a high degree of usefulness in the conduct of intelligence or counterintelligence activities, including analyses to protect against domestic and international terrorism. Hence, it is stipulated that microfilm and other records pertaining to the identity of persons maintaining or authorized to act with accounts be kept since they are of particular value. Once again, it is clear that the government wants to lay its hands (or be able to lay its hands) on each and every bit of information that might be remotely connected to any suspicious activity suggesting terrorism. My question here is: Does this implicate even a business analyst (for example) working at a financial institution, performing analyses with regard to credit risk and modeling, simply because this analyst can potentially maintain consumer accounts? Or, is a credit card advisor at a call center, who acts on dozens of credit card accounts in a day, implicated indefinitely for the activity on a suspicious account? These are valid thoughts and arguments for what exactly the government plans to do with such a vast amount of information gathering. There is no doubt that not only financial privacy is at risk but seemingly all domains of privacy are at risk with the amendments and the legislation of the USA PATRIOT ACT.

The thought of any government agency being able to investigate and collect any information it so desires, raises concerns in my mind of where American society might be headed. It is clear that the government and its agencies have successfully empowered themselves to investigate even the slightest, unrelated suspicion to any degree the government and its agencies deem fit. While the above examples only pertain to financial privacy, they constitute only a small slice of all the potential privacy violations that the USA PATRIOT ACT is capable of committing.

The USA PATRIOT ACT and related efforts of the government to attain national security are appreciated with regard to recognizing the importance of security. There is no doubt about the fact that interest in national security has encouraged interest in information systems security and boosted support and efforts in this space. However, from the perspective of the financial services industry, it is prudent to note that enforcing this kind of security in accordance with legislation, also poses a burden. Notwithstanding the privacy argument, financial institutions are faced with compliance issues, to be fulfilled in phenomenal and challenging time constraints. Many Information Security teams in financial institutions are faced with shortage of resources to fulfill the provisions of the demanding legal landscape.

In the case of the USA PATRIOT ACT, financial institutions have to designate a compliance officer, design a compliance and training program for employees and effectively ensure that the institution is in compliance with respect to the criteria stipulated in the act. Prescribing a minimum compliance process is all well and good. However, no extra resources are provided to the teams that ensure compliance. Typically, it is an added responsibility for somebody designated as the compliance officer and it can prove a burden over and above the normal day-to-day duties of that person working toward achieving information security for his/her institution. It should also be noted that comprehension of such legislature is expected sooner than is realistically possible, given the influx of the latest laws and bills being passed.

It is also interesting to observe that coupled with legislation that enforce security, there are increasing laws and bills being passed to enforce privacy. Examples of such laws and bills are the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and the more recent, California Senate Bill 1386. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act serves to enhance consumer privacy with regard to financial information and it is primarily an act encouraging privacy. However, close examination of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act also shows inherent flaws in the thinking of consumer financial privacy. For example, the act prescribes for financial institutions to use an “opt-out” framework for consumers to protect from disclosure of information, which is being criticized due to the complex nature of the paperwork involved for a consumer to opt out of such disclosure (typically). Another weakness of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act is its inability to enforce transparency and clarity on the part of financial institutions and their privacy policies. In essence, this is a case of time and effort spent in conjuring an inadequate privacy act from the point of view of the consumer. Comparatively, the California Senate Bill 1386, is a bill that truly zooms in on the financial privacy of a consumer. It basically stipulates that any breach of personally identifiable information over unencrypted channels should be reported to a consumer in as expedient a manner as possible. It even defines personally identifiable information as first name and last name in combination with any of a social security number, California Identification number or driver’s license number, or an account number. This is certainly a case of a privacy law that truly has the interest of the consumer at heart and even though it is a State bill, it is hoped that such a bill will propagate and eventually be passed at the Federal level.

The intent of discussing emerging privacy laws is not for analysis or criticism. The question in my mind is, “Are we duplicating effort to design separate security and privacy legislation?” The point I wish to make is that there seems to be unnecessary duplication of effort and time spent in designing laws to deal with issues of security and privacy. On the one hand, there is a lot being done at a Federal level to ensure national security but with a lack of sensitivity toward the issue of privacy. This in turn leads to increasing legislation at the State level to make up for the privacy lost in ensuring security. There is ample evidence of voluntary organizations and groups lobbying to fight for privacy. The treatment of the issues of security and privacy is obviously mixed up and calls for better grounding and awareness of these two different but closely related concepts. I firmly believe that a balance between security and privacy can be reached and this is the challenge for our society as we make advancement in the fields of science and technology.

In conclusion, there is massive effort to promote issues of national security and information systems security at all levels today. A major part of these efforts involve policy-makers, who are entrusted with great responsibility with regard to the privacy of individuals. This paper has provided a small glimpse into issues of financial privacy and how it is being eroded by means of ensuring information security within financial services, motivated by changes in the legal landscape. Moreover, the financial services industry has to bear the brunt of enforcing latest legislation without sufficient guidance or resources. The concepts of security and privacy have to be untangled and looked at separately so as to achieve security, without compromising privacy.
