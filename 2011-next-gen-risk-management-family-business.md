# Next-Gen Risk Management for Family Business

## Introduction

Family-run businesses contribute between 60 and 70 percent of the GDP in most developed and developing countries, including India. According to the Confederation of Indian Industry (CII), only 13 percent of the family businesses survive until the third generation and a mere four percent beyond that. Karvy Private Wealth issued the “India Wealth Report” in September 2010 and their key message is that the wealth held by individuals in India will double in the next three years. Most of these individuals are either owners of family-run businesses or closely associated to the same. Given these high-level characteristics, it hardly needs to be stated that risk management would be a critical competency for family businesses.

Risk management can be described as a continuous process of assessing and mitigating uncertainty to an acceptable level. While risk management spans multiple domains and in the case of some family businesses, multiple lifetimes, the focus of this paper is management of “next generation risks” related to family business. These risks arise as a result of the increasing level of individual and business transactions being done using the Internet and other forms of network communications. Information is considered to be a unit of our society and consequently, a becoming form of global currency. Like dollars and cents crossing transactional boundaries, information traversing the networked world gives rise to the concept of “information risk”. Furthermore, these risks are not immediately recognized and much less understood, therefore making them next generation in nature.

Many financial institutions, defense suppliers, healthcare institutions, and other large organizations have recognized the problem of information risk management. This has led to the creation of products and services that cater to the needs of such “institutionalized” entities. While some of these entities may be forward-looking or strategic, most of them realized the problem only after they were targeted or impacted by a security breach. Same is the case with individuals who may have faced identity theft. The hypothesis is that most Indian family businesses, however, are either unaware of the problem or may have only heard about it through media reports or trade articles.

This paper will describe the information risk management problem by relating to specific scenarios that apply to family business. It will also offer some prescription on how to manage the information risks, once they have been identified. The key message is that information risk management should be prioritized and developed as a core competency for family businesses. By doing so, organizations in this unique segment would possibly help themselves leapfrog into 21st century business leaders. In some cases, mitigating information risks may be the difference between a Rs. 100 crore business and a Rs. 500 crore one.

## Dimensions of Information Risk Management

Information risks (and managing them) are better understood by segmenting the problem into multiple dimensions. The first dimension is Identity, which is fundamental to transacting or taking meaningful part in any online activity. Family members rely on the Internet in an always-on fashion and as a result, maintain a virtual identity. Extending to the business domain, the business itself may have an online presence that needs to be preserved and protected accordingly. The next dimension is Privacy, which is a basic need arising after having establishing identity. Family members conduct business and social interactions in cyberspace, which could lead to unintended leakage of information. For the business, Web 2.0 technologies such as blogs and chat rooms provide a new channel to disseminate non-public information. The third dimension is Security, which technically refers to maintaining confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information. Family members unconsciously assume that information systems are secure - they store and process data without unauthorized disclosure, they maintain the accuracy of information, and they are available for use when needed. In the case of the business and its management, these expectations are taken for granted and necessary conditions for continuous technology operations. The final dimension is Defensibility, which is especially important for family businesses looking to grow in regulated industries. This dimension closely relates to the governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) expectations that the outside world has of the family business. A healthy customer base, positive cash flows, and brand equity can be quickly diminished if the family business is unable to defend an information risk management program to regulators and other interested stakeholders.

While it may be obvious that information risks, like other risks, need to be managed, one should ask why risk management in the first place? For starters, let's talk about risk. While it takes years to get comfortable with risk, one way to think about risk is the chance consequence of something (usually negative) happening. As human beings, we are faced with risk everyday - in some instances our mental decision-making abilities are so fine-tuned that we do not recognize ourselves as being in the risk domain. In other instances (e.g. spending a large sum of money on something), we start to think about the risk dimension more naturally. In quantitative terms, risk can be defined as the product of the potential Likelihood of Impact and the potential Magnitude of Impact. So, risk management is appropriate because we are trying to manage (lower) either the chances or the impact of negative events.

Looked at another way, risk management offers a tried and tested approach for prioritizing limited dollars to simultaneously competing problems. The bottom-line is that there are limited resources at hand to protect identity, assure privacy, ensure security, and provide defensibility all the time. Trade-offs will need to be made by family business owners and professionals as to which information risks matter more and where to allocate resources. A risk management approach can bring out the most critical information risks and enable better decision-making around which risks to address first. Finally, most successful family businesses are managing risks continuously and pervasively, even if they may not be conscious about it all the time. Introducing information risk management can provide a comfortable match to the prevailing culture in successful family businesses. For those families or businesses that do not function using a risk-based approach, information risk management could be a good opportunity to introduce such discipline and extend it to other domains of the family business.

## Identity

The Identity dimension relates primarily to the Family and Business domains in the three-circle model. To understand the risks, let’s consider some potential scenarios. In the case of family business owners, their young children are using the Internet in greater numbers, with greater frequency, and at a younger age. This translates into the creation of online identities at a relatively earlier stage of life as compared to their parents, which also means a longer history of their online behavior and preferences. It is well known that at a young age, children tend to make mistakes such as mixing with the wrong company or experimenting with activities that have a negative connotation. Eventually, the children’s online identity may evolve into something that neither they nor their parents would be proud of, but would nonetheless be available for public consumption. Furthermore, if the children are perceived as de-factor heirs of the business, their tainted identities may spill over into the business domain, leading to a web of reputation risk.

Businesses these days also have online identities, which start as an extension of their brand in the physical world. With the advent of web technology and social media, it is difficult for a business or its brand to ignore the significance of maintaining an online presence. An initial parallel identity gets created in the form of a .com website or a Twitter account, which can evolve unchecked due to the open and complex nature of the Internet. For example, the business owner may not even realize that the brand mark has been copied several times over and used in a phony e-mail message to lure potential customers into visiting a virus-infected website. Or for that matter, the business website may be defaced by miscreants, leading to a considerable drop in customer confidence and deteriorated brand perception. Clearly, the online brand and business identity is an asset that needs to be managed by mitigating these new forms of risk in a connected world.

In both the scenarios described above, there are risk mitigation strategies that can be deployed to protect the underlying identities. In the case of children, prevention is better than cure and the older generation may want to establish some rules governing when and how children get access to the Internet. An educational initiative should be designed by bringing in outside consultants or by enrolling children in elementary computer courses that cover the topic of identity theft. In the case of the business, the brand and its online representation should be entrusted to professionals who are aware of the risks and not just to any “IT guy” who promises cheap services. Whosoever is being engaged must be interviewed thoroughly and asked the tough questions to gauge proficiency. The support staff responsible for maintaining the business’ online presence should be made aware of the business impact of such incidents. They should also be trained to design online brand strategy before simply jumping into putting the business online.

## Privacy

The most common Privacy scenario these days is that involving the popular social networking site Facebook. It seems that everyone wants to be on Facebook to build their friend network, share pictures, and stay in touch. Unfortunately, a lot of people participate in this new medium through uncontrolled or unmanaged ways, leading to dire consequences as concerns their privacy. For example, compiling a list of friends and making it easily available to others can result in stalking or compromise of one’s friends’ identities. So not only is one’s own privacy breached, but friends’ privacy may also be at risk by inadvertent divulging of their personal information. Uploading photos onto such sites can also compromise privacy because what was intended to stay within a group of family/friends may leak out to the public domain if not secured properly. A lot of times the features to control such leakage are obscure, easily missed, or replaced during the next upgrade of the social networking platform. Finally, those tempting one-line status updates can be used to track down one’s physical presence and become knowledgeable of one’s whereabouts. With sufficient motivation, these activities can be leveraged to create a fairly accurate sketch of one’s life and thus cause a conscious discomfort of the loss of privacy.

In the business domain, privacy issues can arise as a result of unchecked or unfiltered communication by insiders with the outside world. The channels that are available to a lot of IT-enabled businesses today are many, including e-mail, web chat, online forums/discussion boards, mobile text messages, and social networking sites. Without a robust set of technical controls, confidential business information can leak through any of these channels. Even if the controls do exist, there is nothing stopping employees from spilling information outside the business/office walls on their personal time, via their personal access mechanisms. This breach of business-level privacy can lead to competitive disadvantages or exploitation of trade secrets against the family business.

In order to mitigate these risks in either the family or the business domain, the underlying theme is that of privacy consciousness. Individuals as well as the business entity should be conscious of their identity and the privacy that should be afforded to that identity. Typically, the most effective way to emphasize privacy is through the negative consequences of the erosion of privacy. For example, a stranger who has learned about a family member’s friends circle and makes it known, would signify the loss of privacy and thus lead to greater consciousness. Similarly, if trade secrets of a business are posted on a publicly accessible website, the business would understand the loss of privacy and gain consciousness. Of course, there are other forms of risk mitigation such as in-built privacy controls in the technology being used by family members and business stakeholders alike. For example, staying educated about Facebook’s privacy features and applying them can provide reasonable protection. Similarly, on the business front, solutions are available to detect and prevent information leakage from the business/office boundaries to the Internet. Cyber surveillance services are also available to monitor for company confidential information.

## Security

In today’s information-based society, both individuals and entities are increasingly dependent on information systems and their seamless operation. As described earlier, a broad view of security would include confidentiality, integrity, and availability objectives for information systems. Individual family members are using more and more devices, which are everyday information systems in compact form factors. For example, data that originates at a desktop or laptop computer gets transferred to a mobile smart phone, which then gets transmitted wirelessly to another network altogether. In this sense, individuals assume security not just of their personal computers but all the peripheral devices that handle the flow of information. Furthermore, in the web-based world of services ranging from online banking to electronic shopping, security is assumed and provides the confidence for individual family members to transact online.

From a business perspective, owners and management rely on unprecedented levels of management information systems to review performance, understand trends and make decisions. Once again, the security of the underlying system is of utmost importance and a critical requirement for continuous operations. To illustrate the impact of the lack of security measures for a business, let’s consider a business with substantial e-commerce facility. If the underlying system were not secure, an attacker could exploit this weakness in the system and bring it down, preventing it from being used by customers. Not only would this lead to an increase in missed sales opportunities and lost revenues, it would also result in a weakened level of confidence in the business. Furthermore, an opportunistic competitor could capitalize on such an incident to drive business away and cause irreparable damage.

The greatest risk mitigation available from the security standpoint is good design and ongoing maintenance of information systems. An information system or technology solution must be designed keeping the intended use in mind. For example, if a smart phone is being purchased to do business on the go, then the phone and its features should be analyzed to understand the flow of information and its security. Furthermore, a process (even though it may be informal) must be established to maintain the device, which would include applying any manufacturer updates or bug fixes that may be released after the device has been purchased. Large families may find it beneficial to have an annual awareness session conducted for them, which would cover the latest personal computing technologies and their secure use. Individuals should also seek out cutting-edge concierge services that would manage one’s personal computing for a service fee e.g. applying updates to computers, backing up data, and handling multiple online account credentials.

For business information systems such as a customer contact database or a central accounting system, policies must be defined to control the storage, processing, and transmission of information. Regardless of the size of a family business is, if it is increasingly dependent on information systems, investments must be made to ensure best practices. Information systems should not be set up once and left alone - like a car, they require periodic “tune-ups” in the form of system updates to keep them functioning properly. It would be a good idea to develop an intelligence program that automatically subscribes to best practices and ensures their implementation. In the case of more institutionalized family businesses, it may not hurt to engage the services of a reputed information security consultancy to audit systems periodically and provide recommendations.

## Defensibility

The final dimension of information risk management concerns the Ownership and Management domains of the three-circle model. This dimension applies to mature family businesses, especially those operating in regulated industries and looking to grow. It may also apply to family-run businesses that may convert from being privately-held to raising capital from public shareholders. One consequence of the global financial crisis and other events that marked the last decade, is the increased level of scrutiny. As the world becomes flatter and Indian family businesses aspire to grab a share of the global pie, stringent scrutiny will take place in the form of technical barriers to trade and higher standards of governance. Lack of controls around handling information (as part of broader due diligence) is an easy argument to make in order to prevent or limit the growth of a domestic business.

Family businesses can invest in a formal information risk management program, especially if they operate in industries such as healthcare, financial services, or other service-oriented verticals. By doing so, not only would they protect their business from the risks described throughout this paper, they would also develop a risk-based competence that can be applied in other areas. Independent assurance of information risk management would also lend credibility to family businesses, in some cases, serving as a marketing tool to get new business. International accreditation such as ISO 27001 and 27005 can provide a seal of certification, providing much more confidence to a joint venture partner or potential client looking to do business. Adopting a program-based approach can make the family business practices much more defensible and also provide feedback on the effectiveness of risk mitigation practices.

Once the dimensions have been prioritized, the preceding three-circle models can be used as templates to differentiate focus between the Family, Business, and Ownership domains.

In closing, I would like to propose a “lifecycle approach” to risk mitigation for family businesses. This is illustrated by the diagram below and suggests that any form of information risk management begins with awareness to understand the problem within the context of each unique family business. After stakeholders are made aware, family business professionals can proceed to make a realistic assessment or diagnosis. Based on the results of an assessment, we can move to the advisory phase and provide consulting or prescribe solutions to mitigate information risks. Finally, automation would be reserved for the most mature and ambitious family businesses, which strive to implement a full-scale information risk management program.
