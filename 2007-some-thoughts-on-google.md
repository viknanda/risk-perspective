# Some Thoughts on Google

I read an article in the New York Times (06/03/07) about Google. It is an interesting read because of the tiny glimpse it provides into Google - especially as it pertains to its search algorithms and the tuning that is constantly being performed so that it can “Give me what I want.” As I read the article, many thoughts crossed my mind, which are detailed below. This essay seeks to elaborate these thoughts and concludes with subscription to the idea that indeed, Google is the most powerful company on the Internet. So powerful, that it may just help solve some of the information security problems that we face everyday.

The author of the article describes Google as “most visited, most profitable and arguably the most powerful company on the Internet.” While many people would agree with such a statement, I am not looking to agree or disagree. I think back to eight years ago, when we were almost at the peak of the “dot.com” bubble. I wonder, did anybody make a similar claim about some Internet company back then? Remember Netscape? What about Hotmail that popularized web-based e-mail (webmail), which happens to be one of Google's most utilized application today? Or AltaVista, which seemed to have tremendous fanfare and following (at least in the part of the world where I lived back then and among my circle of friends)?

Speaking of Google's popularity and the “gold standard” identity, how much of it was really a function of time? By this I mean that users could not really find what they were looking for and along came a search engine, with a simple, neat search field and a six-letter word right above it. There is no discrediting the fact that it was the “PageRank” brainchild of Larry Page and Sergey Brin that led to Google's popularity. However, I have a feeling that just as some elements of the Internet became old, an effective algorithm, simple design, increasing user access speeds and a “freshness” catalyzed Google's success. I left one key reason out - read on.

These days, in Web 2.0, there is a subtle but growing sentiment that Google does not always give one what one wants. This is characterized by the efforts of various other start-ups attacking the search problem. For example, Mahalo seeks to establish itself by harnessing the power of human beings building hand-crafted search results. There are many other “alternate” search engines that are using all types of techniques - algorithmic, varied search bases, visual representation - to establish their mark in the field with the belief that they can do it better. Perhaps populous nations such as India and China may be able to throw millions of human resources toward a search solution like Mahalo, wherein each one of us will be able to get instant, high-quality search results. After all, wouldn't this just be an extrapolation of the booming online concierge services that can wake you up on a daily basis, find a parking spot for your rental car at the next vaction spot and find a pet grooming service as well as schedule the appointment?

Regardless of who conquers the search domain or who remains at the forefront of search technology, the problem of search may be defined as the intersection of people and information. It is a human problem. Forget about the terabytes of data that machine has to sift through - by maximizing transistors on a silicon chip and realizing light-speed communications, we will be able to surmount the technical challenge. However, the notion of getting what one wants, is very much a function of individual human minds. Will this lead mankind to finally experience Cronenberg's depiction of plugging a “pod” into a “bio-port” near the bottom of the spine, bridging the human mind and a machine, resulting in quality search results?

How do we define quality, especially as it pertains to information? From the article, “Is it better to provide new information or to display pages that have stood the test of time and are more likely to be of higher quality?” The answer to these questions may have serious consequences for new content producers and possibly limit their extent. Or, alternate solutions (let's say search functionality embedded within a social networking site) could be a boon for “indie” content producers - I liken this to winning an election campaign first, at the grassroots level and then bubbling up to the national level.

Then again, the organizer, the index and (increasingly) the custodian is able to solve the quality problem all by itself. It is probably the largest data store in the world and has unfettered access to what people are looking for. As human beings, we have turned over whatever crosses our minds “freely” to Google. As a Google Fellow remarks, “When there is a blackout in New York, the first articles appear in 15 minutes; we get queries in two seconds,” helping us realize the power that Google has - all thanks to us googling our minds!

One can imagine that an extension of the blackout example is someone who thinks of a novel idea and starts to google certain related keywords to try and do some preliminary research. Who is to say that Google is able to monitor select query streams and rapidly analyze that somebody is up to something novel. After all, a novel idea should not result in many direct hits, which is precisely what search quality teams at Google focus on so as to finetune their technology. Where does that leave an innovator? An innocent one at that - all (s)he was trying to do was a feasibility study or analysis.

All that I am presenting here is an analysis of Google's capability based on my experience with technology and what is written in the article. We can have an endless debate about the consequences of organizing, indexing and storing so much data and whether it is right or wrong. I am not against Google's advent because I, like many others, have benefited from Google's technology. And I appreciate the “openness” to it all - sharing photos, extending the Maps application or allowing “indie” content producers to make some money by displaying relevant ads on their web site. What I really wish to highlight is that Google has picked the right spot and the right problem i.e. the focus is information. If they say that change is the only constant, then in Web n.0, information is the only constant.

I am going to guess that as much as the next Google application launch seems smooth and fun to the rest of the world, top-level Google insiders are just as anxious to ensure their keeping pace with information and its social applications. An interesting observation (stems from the field that I am in) is the information security -related acquisitions that Google has been making of late. While an anti-virus product had always been part of Google Desktop, soon came anti-spyware and in the near future, anti-malware perhaps. The anti-malware piece is more than likely going to be a combination of its related research and its latest acquisition, that hinges on browser virtualization or sandboxing to isolate an online session and limit the potential attack surface.

I strongly believe that realizing the importance of the information security problem, especially as it pertains to the individual users of its technology, emphasizes Google's power. It is power exemplified on multiple fronts - utilizing market capitalization to acquire other companies, utilizing grey matter to perform cutting-edge research and flirting with the idea of solving an essential problem coming in the way of user experience. Only time will tell whether my conjecture holds but all the signals definitely point in that direction. And the more I think about the problem, who better to solve it than someone who has access to troves of data, which may be analyzed at a scale and level that is unattainable in a short period of time (assuming that we desire a quick solution to the problem).

In conclusion, I leave the reader with a final thought. The tenets of information security are confidentiality, integrity and availability. Should there strike a natural disaster or catastrophe that were to impact the availability of information, no fear. Google “...makes a copy of the entire Internet - every word on every page - that it stores in each of its huge customized data centers...” Is Google also going to be the world's disaster recovery plan? You decide.
