# Transaction Cost Implications of UID

## The Promise of UID

The unique identification number (UID) project seeks to break down one of the biggest barriers that prevent India’s poor from accessing services from both, public and private sector agencies. Specifically, identity verification is a pre-requisite to avail the benefits of many services but comes at a price that is often prohibitive for the poor. These transaction costs take two forms: first, the basic cost of owning and maintaining identity documentation and second, the recurring cost associated with multiple verification processes.

The UID project is geared towards creating a mechanism that uniquely identifies a person, and ensures instant identity verification. By creating a centralized identity verification infrastructure that can be extended to service providers for electronic authentication, transaction costs can be brought down on the principle of “collect once, use many”. That is, once a resident’s demographic and biometric profile has been collected in the centralized database, it can be used many times over again to meet the requirements of identity verification for gaining access to services.

## Transaction Cost Applications

There are many situations in which the UID has the potential to lower transaction costs. We will discuss examples from banking, education, and healthcare, all of which include services with a need for identity verification. In the case of banking, the need for identity verification stems from the requirement to reduce risk of fraud and money laundering. In terms of education, identity verification is required for maintaining student records and also for effective measurement of the country’s education-related initiatives. Identity verification is also becoming increasingly important in healthcare - unique identity can facilitate a more efficient process for customized medical treatment as well as privacy of patient records.

Banks are required to follow “Know Your Customer” or KYC guidelines, which include certain customer identification procedures for opening bank accounts. Currently, these procedures depend on physical interaction between the bank and its customers, often taking place at a brick-and-mortar bank branch or through the deployment of verification field staff. Employing the same procedures to provide banking services for the poor, especially living in remote locations, greatly increases the KYC costs for banks. For the potential customers who have access to banking services, producing documentation is costly as it often involves multiple trips and sometimes even harassment from middlemen who get involved in the process. UID can help reduce costs associated with bringing bank accounts to the poor through the implementation of remotely accessible electronic banking procedures. In addition, the UID can be leveraged over and over again to fulfill the banking need of poor residents i.e. high volume of low-rupee amount transactions.

The geographic distribution of employment opportunities creates a supply of migrant labor, who have to periodically re-establish their identities in order to get access to basic services. One aspect of this migration pattern is the impact on access to education for children of migrant labor. Securing admission to schools in a new location means reproducing identity verification documents; in some cases, the underlying requirements may not always be consistent between schools or regions. With UID, the children of migrant labor can be easily admitted to schools in a new location without the hassle of having to re-prove their identities using traditional mechanisms. Furthermore, there is potential for the UID to be used as an educational progress-tracking tool that travels with the student and reduces future transaction costs associated with affording higher education e.g. supplying educational records as part of the application process to multiple schools or colleges.

In the health sector, the need for identity verification may not be so obvious but it is gaining importance as healthcare is a continuous process and not just a point-in-time interaction. Different stakeholders including doctors, clinics or hospitals, insurance agencies, and specialist providers are managing healthcare today. Porting health information between these various stakeholders adds to the transaction costs for patients who have to maintain multiple independent relationships and bear the burden of proof. Sometimes patients may compromise the privacy of their records in order to share information quickly to speed up the treatment process. The UID can be used as a patient identifier, which can be augmented with healthcare management applications that maintain patient records in a secure manner. From the standpoint of a poor resident, he/she can avail health services in a more standardized fashion and certainly cannot be denied access on the basis of inadequate identity proof. Finally, maintaining patient history under the UID can help to ensure the integrity of past treatment information, resulting in more accurate future prescriptions.

## Threats to Transaction Costs

While the UID and its applications down the road seem very promising, its success in reducing transaction costs depends on downstream implementation. The current design of UID is relatively “open”, wherein state-level registrars can appoint enrolment agencies to facilitate identity registration and updating in the centralized database. Without adequate monitoring, enrolment agencies could engage in opportunistic behavior and exploit the helplessness of a resident who wants to obtain a UID. Secondly, the overall security and privacy of the centralized identity infrastructure needs to be a key element of design. If these aspects are not addressed early on, applying “band-aids” post-implementation could greatly increase the cost of operating and using the UID system. The cost of continuous updates to identity information should also be accounted for and kept to a minimum. Participating service providers must be “UID-certified” (and therefore abide by a code of conduct) such that residents are not subject to the hold-up problem of denial of service in the event that identity information needs to be updated. Finally, ongoing certification of procedures related to the UID system can help ensure trust in the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the infrastructure. A long-term approach based on economies of scale can help keep the costs of such auditing low and prevent them from being passed on to residents in the form of hidden transaction costs.
