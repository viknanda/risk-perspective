# Security: Where are We?

## Introduction

Let us look around ourselves for a moment and we will observe plenty of interest and discussion surrounding security. The United States government, civil entities, the private sector, academia and individuals like you and I, are all thinking about security issues to varied degrees; for some it is their primary interest and for most others, security thought is lurking somewhere at the back of their minds. The domain of security discussion in this paper spans national or homeland security and electronic security for which are simultaneously being enhanced today. However, in the quest for security, privacy is being neglected and even eroded. Achieving security and compromising privacy is next to achieving nothing. This paper seeks to walk the reader through ideas of security, lay out the current state of security, illustrate the tension between security and privacy and finally propose a potential roadmap to security.

## The Need for Security

Security has been defined by many sources as a process or a means to protect an entity from risk, danger or attack. National or homeland security is mainly based on the concepts of physical security, which refers to the security of the world around us -protecting ourselves, our homes, our food and other physical objects from danger or attack. Electronic security is based on the concepts of systems security and generates a separate discussion because of the emergence of systems and technology in just about every aspect of life. Moreover, a lot of processes in the world today rely on data and electronic security ensures the protection of such data.

So, why do we need security? A straightforward response to this question could be to imagine the world without security. Think of not being able to walk on a street at night without feeling scared of being mugged or living in your home under the fear of robbery or losing all sensitive and personal data to some hacker who posts it on a website for everyone to see. These are just some of the mild consequences of not having security, either in the physical sense or the systems sense.

Physical security really stems from the notion of ownership [1]. It is because I as an individual own my home that I secure my home to prevent it from being ransacked. It is because we live together as a society and enjoy certain liberties and freedom that we protect ourselves from terrorist attacks. The notion of national or homeland security has become very significant after the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, which are a succinct explanation of the need for physical security. Systems security, on the other hand, is difficult to define, much less achieve. Systems can be characterized as being inherently complex, interacting with one another, having emergent properties and having bugs. “Systems are hard to secure, and complex systems are that much more operose” (Schneier 7). Present day operations and processes rely heavily on systems, which either have access to or store a lot of data and information for which reason they need to be secure. The complexity of systems does not make it any easier to achieve this security. Hence, there is a very dire need for electronic security and the advancement of the security aspect of electronic systems should at least parallel the advancement of the electronic systems themselves.

## Current State of Security

There is no doubt that national security is a top issue for the government right now. Swiftly after the events of September 11, 2001, the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled “an act to deter and punish terrorist acts in the United States and around the world, to enhance law enforcement investigatory tools, and for other purposes” [2]. Titled very appropriately as the USA PATRIOT ACT, it really stands for “Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act of 2001”. At that time, emotions naturally ran high and American society scrambled to hold onto anything that might possibly guarantee some sense of security. There is no doubt that even if society did not embrace any and every method of enforcing national security, the government successfully mechanized instruments ensuring security, tunneled via the legal landscape.

The government also established the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) in its bid to ensure national security, motivated after the events of September 11, 2001 [3]. DHS is known to be the most significant transformation of the U.S. government since 1947, when Harry S. Truman created the Department of Defense by merging various branches of the U.S. Armed forces to better coordinate the nation’s defense against military threats. The formation of DHS has similar basis, wherein President George W. Bush decided that 22 disparate domestic agencies need to be coordinated and merged into one department to protect the nation against threats. The main foci of DHS will be to analyze threats and intelligence, guard borders and airports, protect critical infrastructure and coordinate the response of the nation in the event of future emergencies. Again, it is very clear that the government has prioritized national security as number one on its agenda.

In the interest of promoting national security by mitigating risk of a cyber-terrorist attack, the House of Representatives passed the Cyber Security Research and Development Act on February 7, 2002 [4]. This act is specifically aimed at improving systems and cyber security as a means to ensuring national security. Under the bill, the National Science Foundation (NSF) and the National Institute of Science and Technology (NIST) create centers, programs and grants to cultivate a spirit of research and development of computer security. In addition, the Cyber Security Act also authorizes $880 million over a period of five years to create these programs in order to achieve a better-prepared and coordinated U.S. response in the event of such an attack. One must applaud the acknowledgement of the fact that there was a lack of world-class research being conducted to address cyber security needs and the Cyber Security Act certainly provides a means to achieve this.

Hence, in the view of the government and legislature, national security is the top priority and a lot has been done and is being done to achieve a high level of security on various fronts. From this point of view, how secure are we right now? We are probably never as secure as we would ideally like to be but I think it is safe to say that we are pretty secure as things stand today. As I write this paper, news has broken out that Saddam Hussein, former leader of Iraq and commonly known as “The Dictator”, has been captured by U.S. forces. This could very well be a point of inflexion in terms of the level of security felt by the people of the United States and the rest of the world. However, security is something that is maintained, not just attained.

In terms of the private sector and industry, information security is a big concern. In the case of technology companies such as Microsoft, IBM, or Symantec, information security is a key driving factor for their growth, if not a key revenue generator. Technology companies cannot simply provide hardware or software without the security thought put in place. For non-technology companies, such as Financial Services companies or Pharmaceuticals, information security cannot be neglected since such companies have terabytes of data and information which has to be protected from hackers and attacks. Every C-level executive in any company should at least be familiar with the concept of information security and no company with an e-commerce platform can do without it. Estimates show that for the first time, more than 5% of some industries overall IT budgets will be dedicated to information security - a statistic that should not be taken lightly given that IT spending has gone down considerably in the last few years [5].

Many computer security solutions are available in the market today. These range from software solutions such as anti-virus tools and vulnerability scanners to hardware solutions such as firewalls and intrusion detection systems. The private sector is certainly trying to keep pace with security development catalyzed by the surge in worms, viruses, attacks exploiting malicious code and hacking activities such as Denial of Service attacks. However, the private sector is heavily influenced by major events or catastrophes that take place in the business environment, which have major economic implications. More or less, development and innovation in the private sector are motivated by some sort of economic reward or cost benefit. Consumers are also increasingly demanding more security (especially if they are affected by buggy Microsoft Windows operating systems!) and this has certainly forced some companies to adopt an information security strategy.

Hence, from the point of view of the private sector or industry, there are security solutions available out there and there is plenty being done to help mitigate risk. However, it is crucial to observe that most of the security thought and action is carried out as a reaction to something bad that has happened and caused economic loss to organizations, businesses or consumers. “Security is not a product; it’s a process” (Schneier 395). It is essential to understand electronic systems and focus on a security process for these systems, consisting of risk assessments, policies and prevention. Until the private sector and industry do not reach this stage of security thinking, it will be difficult to be a step ahead of the “bad guys”.

Finally, we look at the state of security thinking within academia. Traditionally, academia has been the space where a lot of research (funded by the government and the private sector) is conducted, which then delivers products and deliverables to the market and society (through the private sector). In terms of issues related to security, I feel that academia is still in a debate and standby phase. Research in the areas of computer security, bioterrorism and policy is definitely taking place at some centers of excellence. However, the academic world seems to be just waking up to the idea of conducting research in the field of security and being funded for it.

Academic departments and researchers are in a “debate” phase wherein they are discussing the ideas of security in their relevant fields. Also, grants for specific research in say, computer security or bioterrorism are just beginning to show up in the offing for research funding. An observation I wish to make is that oftentimes the idea of security goes against the idea of openness and I am not sure whether that too, is playing a role in the relatively slower movement of security discussion and research. To elaborate this point further, take the case of wireless networks at a university campus versus wireless networks at a financial institution or healthcare organization. Wireless networks at a university campus are pretty much open and even broadcast the service set identifier (SSID), a key parameter required for configuration in order to gain access to the network. At a financial institution or healthcare organization, typical security policy will prohibit broadcasting of the SSID. One may argue that a lot more sensitive information trespasses a corporate network as compared to a campus network but it really depends on who categorizes what as being sensitive; I can always argue that my electronic mail or communication trespassing the campus network is sensitive and we should put in even more controls around the network. The point I wish to illustrate is that typically, in an academic or research environment, general networking and computing facilities are somewhat open and in some cases, one need not even be affiliated with the institution to make use of these resources. However, with the recent surge in viruses and attacks and generally increasing security concern among IT departments at universities, the “open” policies are changing. There is a lot of momentum toward setting up an IT Security department and putting in place sound policies to mitigate risk.

David Patterson recently presented an inaugural lecture in the School of Electrical and Computer Engineering’s William A. Anthony Lecture Series entitled, “Future Directions for Computer Engineering” [6]. One of his key points was that engineers in the 21st century have to focus on making more secure and dependable systems. He was even asked by a member of the audience about the “junk” that Microsoft produces and he responded that Microsoft has produce vulnerable software but that there is a massive effort led by the number one man at Microsoft to seriously remove flaws and vulnerabilities in their software and henceforth focus on security more than anything else. He emphasized the point that security cannot be neglected and everyone is making an effort to address it, even companies like Microsoft which have been perceived to neglect security.

However, when I explored the fields of Electrical and Computer Engineering and Computer Science at Cornell, I was dismayed to discover that there is only one professor whose core research is centered on computer security or systems security. I spoke to one of the top professors in the field of semiconductor devices at Cornell and bounced the idea of inherent security at the device level and how many researchers were actively pursuing that; he lamented that until now it has not been important for them to do so and even in the near future, they are too absorbed in their research to really venture out into this field. That is not to say that research is not being conducted in security, it is still not the buzz of some of the leading academic departments of this nation, which at some points in time, have been at the forefront of science and technology. I do not hesitate to remark that a vast number of computer science graduates from Cornell and other peer institutions do not have the necessary grounding in application or database security or even network security! And these are the very graduates who will be out in industry building software and infrastructure. Do you expect it to be very secure? I think a prime testimony to the inadequacies of the curriculum and interest in security at Cornell specifically, is the fact that Cornell is not recognized by the National Security Agency (NSA) as one of the 50 universities designated as Centers of Academic Excellence in Information Assurance Education [7].

Thus, the current state of security in academia is certainly not where it should be. However, we are moving in the right direction by at least recognizing the importance of security and highlighting this fact. The government is a major proponent of security, be it national security or cyber security and the private sector is somewhat forced to allude to this because of economic interests and global demand for improved IT security services in the wake of increasing viruses and attacks. The academic community is just waking up to the call for security research and of course, once the funds start trickling in specifically for security-related research, I am confident that security will become the buzz word of some of the leading academic departments and research groups.

If security is the left-hand side of the equation, then privacy is the right-hand side of the same equation. Privacy is not a simple concept to define. Philip Agre and Marc Rotenberg define it as the following: “Privacy is the capacity to negotiate social relationships by controlling access to personal information.” [8]. This definition is the most encompassing with regard to the concept of privacy and the role it plays in our society. We should also keep in mind the more traditional meaning of privacy, which is the exclusive right an individual has over his/her space, time or other belongings. It is apparent that as human beings who are a part of a complex social system, it is easier for us to understand the idea of invasion of privacy versus privacy itself.

Schneier makes an accurate observation about people and privacy. “People have a complicated relationship with privacy. … Almost no one realizes exactly how important privacy is in his or her life” (Schneier 59). Businesses, on the other hand, know the importance of privacy and sometimes go a long way to protect it. However, when cost-cutting and other measures beckon for sustained economic prosperity “security is the first thing that gets thrown out the window” (Schneier 59). Governments are seemingly comfortable with the idea of privacy in terms of military secrets and the like. Unfortunately, in recent times, especially post 9/11, the notions of security and privacy have been mixed up and it is the individual in society who is paying and will pay the price of the jumbled details of security and privacy. The next part of this paper dissects a section of the USA PATRIOT ACT and demonstrates the compromise of privacy en route to attaining security.

The USA PATRIOT ACT has greatly expanded the authority of the FBI to monitor people living in the United States. It gives the FBI unprecedented access to personal records and belongings for the sake of investigation if somebody is simply suspected of being connected to terrorist activity without the individual even knowing that he or she is being tracked and monitored. In the discussion that follows, the United States Constitution (and its amendments) should be kept in mind. The First Amendment guarantees free speech rights; “Congress shall make no law… abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the government for a redress of grievances” (United States Constitution, First Amendment). The Fourth Amendment guarantees privacy and due process rights; “The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no warrants shall issue, but upon probably cause…” (United States Constitution, Fourth Amendment).

Section 215 of the USA PATRIOT ACT amends the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) of 1978, which essentially gives the FBI unprecedented access to sensitive, personal records and any “tangible things” [9]. The FBI can obtain a Section 215 order against any individual based in part or solely on activities relating to the First Amendment. Apart from personal records, the FBI may go to the extent of monitoring books borrowed from a library, websites visited by an individual, organizations that an individual took part in and track many other aspects of one’s lifestyle to aid in its investigation without even demonstrating “probable cause” or evidence of being involved in terrorist activity. Moreover, a gag order in the law prevents anyone served with a Section 215 order from telling anyone else that the FBI demanded information. It is interesting to note that the gag order remains in effect forever and surveillance targets are never notified that their privacy has been compromised.

Another striking feature of a Section 215 order is the fact that applications for such orders are usually heard by judges of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court (FISC). Congress created the FISC in 1978 to oversee FBI surveillance in foreign-intelligence investigations. It is comprised of 11 district judges who are appointed by the Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court for terms of up to seven years. Moreover, the FISC meets in secret, almost never publishes its decisions and allows only the government to appear before it. An interesting statistic is the following: since 1978, the FISC has heard approximately 15,000 wiretap and electronic surveillance applications (these do not include Section 215 orders since the FBI is not required to report them) of which it has summarily approved all but 5 and it did not reject even one.

Other aspects of an individual’s privacy are also affected by the USA PATRIOT ACT. An interesting amendment relating to financial privacy is that made to the Fair Credit Reporting Act of 1970 [10]. The central theme of this amendment is that disclosures to governmental agencies for counter terrorism purposes will be fulfilled accordingly; any consumer reporting agency shall furnish a consumer report and all other information in a consumer’s file to a government agency authorized to conduct investigations. Why should the government get a hold of my consumer report? It is awfully suggestive that by seeking even consumer reports, the government plans to map the most remote clues available to terrorist activity if possible, all in the bid for national security. Another disturbing feature of this amendment is the fact that an individual will never know that the government requested his or her consumer report. My analysis of such an amendment only leads me to think that such information-gathering implies immense tracking and monitoring of consumer habits and a gross invasion of an individual’s financial privacy; it is scary to think of the possibility of a government agency mapping some “deviant” consumer activity to terrorism and the consequences it could have for someone whose intent does not present any danger or risk whatsoever. I also wonder if a consumer’s report will be hurt because of the simple fact that a government agency requested his or her consumer report. This is a valid question since the USA PATRIOT ACT does not address such issues and deliberately leaves them open-ended. It is this very approach toward security that is violating and gradually eroding the broader concepts of privacy in our society.

Hence, the examples above demonstrate the impact that new security-related legislation has on individual privacy. The government has been swift and proactive to identify national security as its top priority and constructed many tools and mechanisms to aid the war against terror and ensure national security. However, little if any thought, went into drafting these policies with regard to individual privacy. While there is massive effort to promote issues of national security and systems security at all levels today, we should realize that a major part of these efforts involve policy-makers, who are entrusted with great responsibility with regard to the privacy of individuals.

## Potential Roadmap for Security

Now that I have outlined the need for security, described the current state of security and illustrated the tension between security and privacy, this section attempts to propose a potential roadmap for security. This roadmap comprises of two suggestions which might be worth considering in the quest for attaining security. My first suggestion is one that has surfaced before but I still have not seen much attention drawn to it. My second suggestion relates more to technological and scientific exploration of current research, though it remains for the experts to determine its feasibility.

First and foremost, I propose awareness. Awareness is the key to understanding the concepts of security and grounding in the concepts of security will automatically ensure an increased level of security. Physical security is relatively easier to understand and in some sense is imbibed in us right from childhood; our parents telling us to stay safe or we form the habit of locking our doors. Such teachings establish the framework for the bigger picture of national security. As a society we understand the heightened need for security post 9/11 and as such do not have much complaint against increased security spending (what we complain about is the erosion of our privacy in the process of achieving security!). However, the concepts of security do not stop just at physical security - they have to be extended to systems security as well. The fact of the matter is that all of us, in one way or another, are linked to some system that we either operate or that has a good amount of personal information. Systems are the backbone of our daily lives and hence, we need to understand the importance of systems security and achieve it and maintain it. Awareness constitutes realizing the importance of strong passwords and ensuring them in our daily lives, destroying or shredding credit card receipts or bank statements before disposing them, excluding confidential information in the content of electronic mail, prohibiting sharing of usernames or access cards and numerous other measures which will be useful in improving the overall state of security around us.

Universities and colleges should roll up their sleeves and accept it as a responsibility to imbibe at least their graduates with some grounding in the concepts of security, if not their staff and faculty. The buck should not just stop there, but Computer Science and similar departments should delve even further in equipping their graduates with essential security skills to foster a spirit of security thinking and process, which can further be leveraged when these graduates enter the real world. Teaching students the theory behind algorithms, software engineering and computer networks is good but without addressing security or imbibing security thought, such technologies will eventually present more risk and inconvenience than achieving efficiency and productivity. This process will certainly take time and involve revamping syllabi and curriculums but this has to be done if top universities wish to remain at the forefront of science and technology and more importantly, actively contribute toward ensuring security. I am sure Cornell does not wish to be recognized as a university churning out graduates who can code brilliantly (almost like machines!) but who cannot develop secure computing and networking technologies.

My second suggestion relates to engineering and technology research. I think that we should think of security at the device level and as an inherent feature of technology rather than implementing it as another layer on existing technology. I am sure that there are researchers already looking into this but more funding and initiative needs to be pumped into this space. Perhaps its time that Intel pause Moore’s Law in the context of its microprocessing chips and applies it to the context of enhancing security. Won’t it be great if new technologies become doubly secure every couple of years rather than doubling the number of transistors on a chip? This may be achieved by keeping the processing power constant for a while and bringing security onto the chip, making it inherently secure. From there, the microprocessing capability and security capability of the chip could be synergized and Moore’s Law could be applied to both! I do not know what is involved technologically in bringing security to the device level but I think it is a good topic for research. This idea stems mainly from the observation that a lot of security solutions today are in reaction to something bad and hence involve more detection and reaction than prevention. It would be nice if we could focus some research and intelligence toward making good preventive mechanisms and solutions.

## Conclusion

We are on the right track with regard to security and the least is that we have recognized the importance of national security and electronic security. The government is spending billions of dollars in ensuring national security and a portion of this will trickle down to academic institutions and research centers to enhance security solutions and thinking for the 21st century, which will be brought to the mainstream by the private sector. However, the government must realize that its legislative instruments to ensuring national security are inadequate in terms of addressing privacy concerns - they have neglected them completely! I strongly believe that awareness and innovation will help us make progress with regard to ensuring systems security and physical security.
